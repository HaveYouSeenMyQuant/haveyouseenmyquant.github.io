Two losing games that make a winner

paradoxposted 2 Aug 2026

Two gambling games. Each one loses you money on its own. Play them in a random order and you get rich.

£100 becomes £131.60.

GAME ONE is one coin. Heads you win a pound, tails you lose one. It comes up heads 49.5 times in 100 — a hair under fair — so it bleeds you 1p a round.

GAME TWO checks your balance first. On a multiple of three you flip a terrible coin (wins 9.5 in 100); on anything else, a great one (wins 74.5 in 100). Sounds like a winner. It isn't: alone it loses 0.87p a round.

Now pick between the two at random every round. Same coins, nothing added. The money climbs, at 1.57p a round. Over 2,000 rounds: game one leaves £80.00, game two £83.16, the mix £131.60.

WHY. Watch what game two's GREAT coin does when it wins: it moves you up one — and one above "two past a multiple of three" IS a multiple of three. Its good coin keeps feeding you back onto the multiples, where the terrible coin waits. Alone, it walks into its own trap, sitting on a multiple 38.4% of the time instead of the 33.3% an even spread gives.

That 5-point excess is the whole difference. Give those same coins an even spread of balances and they'd EARN 5.67p a round; give them the spread game two itself produces and they lose 0.87p.

Game one ignores your balance, so it keeps knocking you off those multiples. The share drops to 34.5% — the winning side of the line.

Worth knowing: strict alternation does NOT work here. One, two, one, two loses 0.67p a round; a fixed schedule can lock onto the very rhythm that traps game two. Random wins, two-of-each wins.

Checked, not asserted. Only balance-mod-3 matters, so each game is a 3-state chain; exact rational arithmetic gives the rates as −1/100, −73443/8446300 and +223/14200 — both strictly losing, the mix strictly winning. The curves on screen are exact expected balances, and a 120,000-player simulation agrees.

The name, if you want to read on: Parrondo's paradox.
