How much water gets through this network of pipes?

graphsposted 2 Aug 2026

Two pipes feed the top of this network and they can carry twenty between them. Far less than twenty comes out.

Every pipe has its own limit — that is what the width and the number both say. Water goes in at the top, it has to come out at the bottom, and the question is the simplest one you can ask: how much can get through at once?

Almost everyone adds up the pipes at the source. That gives 20. Some add the pipes at the drain instead, which gives 22. Both are badly wrong, and the video shows you why without saying so: when the flow finally stalls, the two pipes at the top are still not full. They never fill. Whatever is stopping the water is not up there.

Here is the rule that actually decides it.

Draw a line splitting the network into a top side holding the inlet and a bottom side holding the outlet. Any line at all — straight, wobbly, whatever you like. Add up the limits of the pipes it crosses on the way down. Every drop that reaches the outlet has to cross that line on one of those pipes, so nothing more than that total can ever get through.

That holds for every line you could possibly draw. So the tightest line you can find is a ceiling on the answer.

And the ceiling is never loose. The most that can flow always equals EXACTLY the tightest cut you can make — not approximately, not usually. Always, for every network of pipes anyone can build. So the problem stops being an optimisation and becomes a hunt for one narrow slice.

Here that slice is nowhere near either end. Watch which four pipes go solid gold when the flow stalls. Those four are the cut, their four numbers are the whole answer, and not one of them touches the source or the drain.

Add them up, then check yourself against the pinned first comment — full working there, including a brute-force sweep over all 128 ways of splitting this network in two and the proof that only one of them is tightest.
