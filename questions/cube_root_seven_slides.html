Four slides down a curve pin the cube root of 7 to 21 decimals

optimizationposted 2 Aug 2026

Nobody can tell you the number whose cube is seven. Four slides down a curve can.

21 decimal places. From a starting guess of "two".

Here is the whole method, and it needs no cleverness at all.

Draw the curve y = x³ − 7. The number you want is where it crosses zero. Guess anything — two is the obvious one, because two cubed is eight. Go up to the curve at your guess, and draw the straight line that touches it there and leans exactly the way the curve leans. Slide down that line until it hits zero. Wherever it lands is your next guess. Go up to the curve again. Repeat.

That is it. And the number of correct decimals it buys you goes 1, 2, 5, 10, 21.

It DOUBLES every time. Count the leading zeros of the relative error at each step: 1.34, 2.71, 5.42, 10.84, 21.68. Divide each by the one before: 2.019, 2.000, 2.000, 2.000. Two, dead on, for as long as you care to keep going.

The reason is in the picture. Close to the answer, the straight line you are sliding down and the curve are almost the same line. The only thing separating them is the curve's bend, and the bend acts on the SQUARE of how far off you are. So each slide squares the error, and squaring a small number doubles the run of zeros in front of it. Zeros in the error are correct digits in the answer.

That is also why the video stops plotting where the guess is and starts plotting how far off it still is. After the second slide the guess lands inside the width of the drawn line and there is nothing left to see on the curve — so the back half is a ruler, one rung per correct decimal, and each fall down it is exactly twice the last.

The catch, said honestly: it only doubles once you are close. Start where the curve is flat and the line you follow is nearly horizontal, and it will fling you miles away.
