Cut the biggest square off this rectangle, again and again

number_theoryposted 2 Aug 2026

Two numbers that look like they share nothing at all. Five squares later, out falls the length that measures both.

187

Here is the whole thing, and there is no arithmetic in it beyond "which side is longer".

Draw a rectangle 1496 by 935. Cut off the biggest square that fits — that's a 935 square, because 935 is the shorter side. What's left is 561 by 935. Do it again: cut off a 561 square. What's left is 561 by 374. Again: a 374 square. What's left is 187 by 374. Again: a 187 square. And what's left is 187 by 187 — already a square. Stop.

Five squares: 935, 561, 374, 187, 187.

The side of that last square is the largest whole length that fits into BOTH starting sides a whole number of times. 1496 is eight of them. 935 is five of them. Nothing bigger works, and you can check that by hand: the only lengths that go into both 1496 and 935 are 1, 11, 17 and 187.

Why it has to come out that way: any length that measures both sides of a rectangle also measures the square you cut off and the rectangle you're left with — you only ever subtracted one from the other. So the set of lengths that measure both sides never changes as you cut. At the end you're holding a single square, and the biggest length that measures a square is its own side. That side has been the answer the whole time.

That also means every square in the picture is a whole number of 187s — 5, 3, 2, 1 and 1 of them — so forty copies of the last square tile the entire rectangle with nothing left over.

Try it with two numbers that share nothing, like 89 and 55. Same procedure, but the squares march all the way down and the last one has side 1. "They share nothing" stops being a claim you take on trust and becomes something you can watch happen.

Two and a bit thousand years old, by the way, and still the fastest way anyone knows to do this.
