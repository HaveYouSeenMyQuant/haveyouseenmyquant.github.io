A checker that can lie yes, but never lie no

cs_systemsposted 2 Aug 2026

A checker that can lie "yes", but can never lie "no".

You want to know whether something is in a set of fifty million things, without storing the set.

So you don't. You keep one row of bits, all dark. To add an item, run its name through a handful of scramblers — each one turns the name into a position — and light those positions. To ask whether something is in there, scramble it the same way and look at the positions it points to.

If any of them is dark, it is definitely not in the set. Nothing could have lit that bit except the item itself, and it never did.

If all of them are lit, it is probably in the set — but only probably. Other items may have lit those exact positions between them, and the row cannot tell you who lit what. So the honest answer is "maybe".

That asymmetry is the whole design, and it is why the thing is useful: you put it in front of an expensive lookup, and you only pay for the maybes. Every "no" is free and certain.

The interview question is the sizing. Given how many items you have and the false-maybe rate you can live with, how long does the row need to be — and how many scramblers should you use?

Both have clean closed forms, and both are more surprising than people expect. The optimal number of scramblers doesn't depend on how many items you have. Neither does the bits-per-item. And "use as many scramblers as you can afford" is wrong — more scramblers catch more missing items, but they also fill the row faster, so past a point you are making false maybes more likely, not less.

Everything in the video is measured, not asserted: a real bit array, real hashing, 140,000 queries for things that were never inserted. Predicted false-maybe rate 1.004%, measured 1.031%, standard error 0.027%. False negatives across the whole run: zero, as promised.
