Twelve exams, some clash — how few time slots do you need?

graphsposted 2 Aug 2026

Twelve exams, some pairs clash. How few time slots do you need? Nobody solves this one in their head.

Twelve exams have to be timetabled. A line between two of them means at least one student is sitting both, so those two can never run at the same time. Give every exam a colour — one colour is one time slot — so that no line ever joins two exams of the same colour. The question is the smallest number of colours that works.

A FLOOR. Find three exams that all clash with each other — the video shades one. No two of them can share, so between them they need three colours. Nothing elsewhere in the picture drags the total below three.

A CEILING. Colour the exams one at a time, each taking the first colour none of its neighbours is already using. No exam here clashes with more than four others, so at most four colours can ever be blocked when its turn comes — a fifth is always free. Five is guaranteed enough.

Then watch that pass run. In reading order, top to bottom, left to right, it paints itself into a corner on the twelfth and last exam: its four neighbours carry four different colours between them, nothing is left, and it opens a fifth. Five colours, on a picture whose floor is three.

Here is what makes it a real question. That method isn't wrong, it's order-dependent. I ran twenty thousand random orders on this instance and 95.2% finish in fewer colours than reading order does — but you only learn that afterwards, and no rule tells you which orders are good without solving the thing first.

At this size you can still brute-force it — I checked all 531,441 three-colour assignments and all 16,777,216 four-colour ones. A few more exams and that stops being possible, and nobody knows a method that stays quick.

So — how few? To be sure you need both: a colouring that works, and a reason nothing smaller can.
