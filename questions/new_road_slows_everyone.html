A new road that makes everybody's drive longer

paradoxposted 2 Aug 2026

65 minutes became 80.

The town: forty drivers, one start, one finish, two ways across. Each way is a wide road that always takes 45 minutes however busy, then a narrow road that takes a minute for every car using it. Mirror images, so traffic splits 20/20, each narrow road costs 20 minutes, every drive is 20 + 45 = 65. Nobody gains by switching sides — you'd only make your new narrow road busier than the one you left.

Now join the far end of one narrow road to the near end of the other. Free, instant.

That lets you skip BOTH wide roads and use BOTH narrow ones: 20 + 0 + 21 = 41 minutes instead of 65. You'd be mad not to.

So is everyone else. Each driver who joins makes both narrow roads a minute slower — and switching still pays at every step, right to the end. With all forty on it, both narrow roads carry 40 cars: 40 + 0 + 40 = 80 minutes each.

Nobody wants to leave, either. Peel off onto a wide road and you pay 40 + 45 = 85. Worse. So all forty stay, 15 minutes a day worse off than before the road existed, and not one of them made a mistake.

Bulldoze it and they all speed up again. Cities have seen exactly this: Seoul tore out an elevated motorway in 2003 and traffic improved.

Checked, not asserted. All 861 ways to split 40 drivers across the three routes were enumerated and every single-driver deviation tested: exactly one split is stable, and it is all-forty-on-the-middle at 80 minutes. 2,000 simulations of drivers greedily re-routing one at a time landed there every time. The exact minimiser of the potential function agrees. Every number on screen is read off the same schedule the cars are drawn from.

The name, if you want to read further: Braess's paradox.
